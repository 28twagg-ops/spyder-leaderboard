# Ginger, probiotics, creatine and supplement quality

**“Natural” covers products with very different evidence. Ginger has symptom trials. Creatine remains investigational in pregnancy.**

**Safety by stage.** Culinary intake differs from concentrated products. Do not infer rare fetal safety from short trials. Report product, timing and symptoms to clinician. Third-party testing addresses aspects of product quality, not clinical benefit.

## Practical choice

For nausea, discuss options with prenatal care. If unable to keep fluids down, seek prompt medical assessment. Review concentrated extracts and supplements individually.

## Effort and cost

Ginger symptom trials lasted four days or three weeks. Product standardization and dose differ.

## What the research measured

Ginger trials in early pregnancy measured nausea over days or weeks. Vitamin B6 equivalence is a different comparison from placebo. Probiotic trials vary by strain, risk group and indication. A GDM-prevention review flagged preeclampsia, whereas a broader review found an imprecise estimate nearer no difference. Creatine energy-buffering mechanisms and a small open-label report cannot establish perinatal clinical benefit.

Limited support for short-term nausea relief. Conflicting preeclampsia evidence and uncertain GDM prevention. Insufficient outcome and safety evidence for routine prenatal creatine.

- **Double-masked placebo RCT** · Early pregnancy with nausea and vomiting. Ginger vs placebo over four days. Nausea improved in 28/32 vs 10/35. Short symptom trial, inadequate for rare fetal outcomes. [S099](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/11275030/)
- **Randomized equivalence trial** · Pregnant before 16 weeks with nausea or vomiting. Ginger vs vitamin B6 over three weeks. Average nausea difference 0.2 with 90% CI −0.3 to 0.8. Equivalence design and 90% interval; do not label it a 95% interval. [S100](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/15051552/)
